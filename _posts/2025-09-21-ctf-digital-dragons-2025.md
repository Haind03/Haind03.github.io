In September 2025 I spent a weekend in Da Nang for Digital Dragons, The Cybersecurity Challenge 2025. We played under the name BlueCyber_Intern, the competition ran on September 20, and on the 21st we walked up on stage at Vietnam-Korea University of Information and Communication Technology (VKU) to pick up a consolation prize. On paper it's the smallest prize on the board. It didn't feel small to me at all, and this post is mostly about why.

## First, thank you BlueCyber

Before anything else, I want to thank BlueCyber. The team name wasn't a joke, it was a statement of where we came from. BlueCyber backed us for this trip and made it possible for us to show up in Da Nang, focus on the competition and not worry about everything around it. A lot of what I know about reversing malware and hunting threats I learned at BlueCyber, first as an intern and later full time, so getting to carry the company's name into a national contest meant a lot. I hope the result made the bet worth it.

## The contest

Digital Dragons is a Jeopardy-style CTF organized by evvolabs, VKU and the National Cybersecurity Association (NCA). The idea behind it is simple and I like it: challenges that look like real-world exploitation instead of puzzles for their own sake, aimed at finding and training the next people who will do this for a living. The prize board literally calls it a "talent search for information security", and the room was full of teams who clearly took that seriously.

## The fight

Jeopardy CTFs have a rhythm, and this one hit every beat. The first hour is a sprint: everyone opens the board at once, grabs the easy points in their own category, and the scoreboard reshuffles every few minutes. I went straight for the reversing and forensics challenges, which is where I'm most useful, while the others split the rest between them.

Then comes the middle of the day, which is where contests are actually decided. The easy flags are gone, every team has a few challenges that refuse to fall, and you start making uncomfortable choices. Do you keep hitting the binary you've already sunk two hours into, or drop it and help a teammate on something that might be closer? We made both kinds of calls that day, and not all of them were right. There's a specific feeling when a flag you were sure about gets rejected, and you have to go back and admit the assumption you built everything on was wrong. We had a few of those.

What I'll remember most is the last stretch. By then our ranking was hovering right around the edge of the prize places, close enough to see it and far enough that one more team's solve could push us out. Every refresh of the scoreboard was a small heart attack. We kept pushing until the very end, because in a contest that tight a single flag decides whether you go home with something or with a story about almost.

## The prize

We went home with something. When they called BlueCyber_Intern for the consolation prize at the closing ceremony the next day, it was honestly relief before it was happiness. Standing on that stage with the other teams and the board in my hands, I wasn't thinking about the teams above us. I was thinking about the stretch in the afternoon when it could have gone the other way.

## Thanks

Besides BlueCyber, thank you to the organizers, evvolabs, VKU and the NCA, for a well-run contest and for bringing it to Da Nang, and to the sponsors Agribank, AGEST, BIDV and Sun World Ba Na Hills for supporting it. Thanks also to ISP Club at PTIT for the shoutout after the closing ceremony. And thank you to my teammates for a long, loud, stressful and very fun day.

## What I took home

Besides the board, I took home a few lessons I keep relearning. Deciding when to drop a challenge is a skill, and it's the one I'm worst at. A team that talks constantly beats a team of strong people working in silence. And small prizes in strong fields are worth more than they look, because the gap between the top of the scoreboard and the edge of the prize zone is often just one solve. Next time I want to be the one who finds it.
